Why You Need an Architect
Before Buying in Spain

What a standard survey misses — and what the right assessment catches. Written by the architects who do this every day.

March 2024 12 min read Updated November 2024

You've found a property you like. Your lawyer has done the legal checks. The estate agent says everything is in order. So why do you need an architect?

Because lawyers check title and contracts. Estate agents check that they can sell. Neither is qualified or incentivised to tell you that the terrace extension was built without a licence, that the roof has 18 months before it needs replacing, or that the renovation you're planning is prohibited by local heritage rules. That is what an architect's assessment does.

We've been doing this on the Catalan Coast for years. This article explains what we look for, what we find, and why the €2,000–€4,000 cost of an assessment is invariably one of the best investments you make in the whole process.

Who this guide is for: International buyers purchasing a second home or investment property in Spain — particularly on the Costa Brava, Maresme, or Costa Dorada — where older properties, rural classifications, and heritage protections create specific technical risks that differ from purchases in the buyer's home country.

What a Standard Survey Misses

Most buyers commission a standard survey (tasación) or, if using a mortgage, rely on the bank's valuation report. These are primarily financial instruments. They establish market value. They do not comprehensively assess structural condition, building legality, renovation constraints, or future costs.

The gaps this creates are significant:

  • Structural problems — cracks, water infiltration, subsidence, failing foundations, deteriorating roof timbers. These require architectural and structural engineering expertise to identify and cost.
  • Illegal construction — extensions, pool houses, terraces, and even entire floors built without municipal licence. These may need to be demolished or legalised at your cost.
  • Planning violations — building on land classified as non-buildable, exceeding permitted floor area, structures within coastal protection setbacks. Some violations carry unlimited liability.
  • Heritage and environmental restrictions — that may prevent you from making changes you assumed were possible.
  • Renovation costs — a property priced to reflect its need for work may cost far more to renovate than the purchase price difference justifies.

The Costa Brava context: Much of the most desirable property on the Catalan Coast was built or extended during periods of relaxed planning enforcement (1960s–1990s). Illegal extensions and undeclared works are extremely common. What looks like a beautiful, fully formed house may have significant illegal elements that become your problem the moment you sign.

What Our Assessment Covers

1. Structural integrity

We examine the load-bearing structure from foundations to roof — walls, slabs, beams, and timber elements. We identify active cracks versus settled ones, water infiltration paths and their sources, evidence of historic or ongoing subsidence, and the condition of the roof structure and waterproofing. We give you a clear condition rating and cost estimate for any remedial work.

2. Building legality

We obtain and review the official building licence and compare it to the property as built. Any discrepancy — an undeclared room, an expanded terrace, a pool added without permit — is flagged. We then advise on whether the illegal element can be legalised (and at what cost), must remain undeclared (creating future risk), or must be demolished.

This is perhaps the most important check we do. In our experience, around 40% of older properties on the Costa Brava have some element of unlicensed construction. Most are minor. Some are not.

3. Land classification and planning constraints

We check the POUM (urban planning map) for the municipality, identify the land classification, and review any applicable coastal, agricultural, or environmental protections. This tells you what can be built, extended, or altered in the future — and what cannot.

We also check whether any pending administrative proceedings or planning infractions have been initiated against the property. These are not always visible in title searches.

4. Renovation feasibility

If you intend to renovate, we assess what is technically and legally possible and provide a realistic cost estimate. We distinguish between what you want to do, what you can do under current planning rules, and what the structure will allow.

This is often where the most value is created. We have had clients walk away from properties where their planned renovation was impossible — and redirected them to properties where it was straightforward. We have also had clients proceed with more confidence knowing that their vision was entirely feasible.

5. Services and systems

We check the electrical installation, water supply and drainage, septic tank or mains connection status, and heating systems. Older properties frequently have undersized or non-compliant electrical systems. Rural properties may have water supply issues that are not apparent from a casual visit.

6. Energy performance

Spain requires an energy performance certificate (certificado de eficiencia energética) for property sales. We assess the property against this requirement and identify any measures that would significantly improve performance and reduce future operating costs.

What Happens When We Find Problems

Finding problems is not the same as killing a deal. The question is always: what does this problem cost, and how does that affect the negotiation?

In practice, our reports typically lead to one of four outcomes:

  1. Proceed as planned. The property is in good condition, legally clean, and the renovation vision is feasible. Our report gives the buyer confidence to move forward.
  2. Negotiate the price. We identify issues that the seller may not have disclosed — or may not even be aware of. The buyer uses our cost estimate to negotiate a price reduction that reflects the remedial work required.
  3. Make the fix a condition of sale. In some cases, it's appropriate to require the seller to resolve an issue before completion — particularly for planning infractions that carry ongoing liability.
  4. Walk away. Occasionally the problems are fundamental. The cost of remediation exceeds the value it would create, or the planning violations are too severe to accept. In these cases, walking away is the right outcome — and our report has saved the buyer from a very expensive mistake.

Real example: A Dutch client asked us to assess a €650,000 stone house in the Begur area. The house was beautiful. During our assessment, we discovered the entire upper floor — two bedrooms and a bathroom — had been added without licence and was built partly on land classified as non-buildable forest. The seller was unaware. The cost to resolve the planning situation was estimated at €45,000–€80,000 with no guarantee of success. Our client negotiated a €60,000 price reduction and proceeded with full knowledge of what they were taking on.

How Our Assessment Works in Practice

The process typically takes 3–5 working days from instruction to report delivery:

  1. Instruction. You share the property details. We agree scope and fee.
  2. Document review. We obtain the building licence, cadastral record, energy certificate, and planning maps before the site visit.
  3. Site visit. One of our architects visits the property — typically 2–4 hours depending on size and complexity. We photograph, measure, probe, and take samples where appropriate.
  4. Report preparation. We produce a written report with photographic evidence, condition ratings, cost estimates, and a clear recommendation.
  5. Call to discuss. We walk through the findings with you, answer questions, and advise on next steps.

If you're not able to be at the site visit in person (which is often the case with international buyers), we send photographs and can conduct a video call from the property to share what we're seeing in real time.

The Cost of Not Doing This

Our assessment fee ranges from €1,800 to €3,500 depending on the size and complexity of the property. That is a small fraction of the transaction value — and a very small fraction of what problems left undiscovered can cost.

We have documented cases where buyers who skipped the assessment faced:

  • Structural remediation costs of €40,000–€150,000 on properties where the problems were visible but not understood
  • Forced demolition of illegal extensions with retrospective fines
  • Planning infractions that made the property unsaleable without resolution
  • Renovation projects that cost 2–3x the original estimate because the structural starting point was poorly understood

We are not trying to make the process harder. We are trying to make it safer. The Catalan Coast is an exceptional place to own property. Done correctly, these purchases bring enormous pleasure and often strong returns. Done carelessly, they can become the most expensive mistake a buyer makes.

Your pre-purchase checklist

  • Commission an architectural assessment before making a firm offer
  • Ask the seller for the original building licence and any subsequent extension permits
  • Verify the cadastral record matches the property as it stands
  • Check the land classification (urban, developable, non-buildable)
  • Confirm any pending planning infractions or administrative proceedings
  • Review the community statutes if purchasing in a development
  • Get a realistic renovation cost estimate before factoring it into your offer
