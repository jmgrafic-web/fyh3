Buying Property in Spain
from the Netherlands

What Dutch buyers need to know — from NIE number to tax obligations in both countries. A practical, step-by-step guide.

Updated January 2025 15 min read

Dutch buyers are among the most active international purchasers on the Catalan Coast. The reasons are straightforward: direct flights from Amsterdam and Eindhoven to Girona and Barcelona, a relatively strong tradition of second-home ownership, and a cultural affinity with the Spanish lifestyle that makes the transition feel natural.

But buying across borders is more complex than buying at home. This guide explains the process, the Dutch-specific tax and legal considerations, and what to do in what order.

Note: This guide addresses the Spanish side of the purchase. Dutch tax implications (Box 3, hypotheekrenteaftrek, etc.) vary by individual circumstance. We strongly recommend consulting a Dutch belastingadviseur with cross-border expertise. This is not legal or tax advice.

Step by Step: The Purchase Process

1

Define your criteria and budget

Before engaging anyone in Spain, be precise about what you want. Region, property type, condition (ready to move in vs renovation project), budget including taxes (add 12–15% to the purchase price), and intended use (holiday home, rental investment, eventual primary residence). Clarity here saves months.

2

Get your NIE number

A NIE (Número de Identificación de Extranjero) is required to purchase property, open a bank account, and pay taxes in Spain. Dutch citizens can apply at the Spanish consulate in Amsterdam, The Hague, or via a Spanish lawyer with a power of attorney (considerably easier). Allow 4–8 weeks. This should be your first administrative step.

3

Appoint a Spanish lawyer

Separate from the estate agent and from us. Your lawyer represents only you — reviewing contracts, checking title, verifying there are no debts or liens on the property, and handling the escritura (deed). Many Dutch buyers use a Spanish lawyer who speaks Dutch; alternatively, a good English-speaking Spanish lawyer is standard. Budget €2,000–€6,000 depending on transaction complexity.

4

Open a Spanish bank account

Required for the purchase transaction, and for paying ongoing costs (IBI, utilities, community fees). Most major Spanish banks (Sabadell, CaixaBank, BBVA) accommodate non-resident accounts with NIE. Some Dutch banks with Spanish presence (like ING) can help facilitate this.

5

Commission an architectural assessment

Before making a firm offer on any resale property, have it assessed by an independent architect. Dutch buyers in particular tend to underestimate the risk of illegal construction and the structural complexity of older Catalan properties. Our assessment costs €1,800–€3,500 and typically saves multiples of that in negotiation leverage or avoided mistakes.

6

Make an offer and sign the contrato de arras

The contrato de arras is a private purchase agreement. You pay a deposit — typically 10% — which is forfeit if you withdraw without cause. If the seller withdraws, they pay double. Your lawyer must review this carefully before you sign. Ensure it includes all relevant conditions (subject to clean title, subject to architectural assessment, etc.).

7

Complete at the notary

The escritura pública is signed before a Spanish notary. Both parties are present (or represented by power of attorney). The balance of the purchase price is transferred — typically by certified cheque or bank transfer. You receive the keys and the signed deed.

8

Pay taxes and register

Within 30 working days, your gestor pays ITP (10% in Catalonia) to the regional government and registers the property in the Land Registry in your name. Keep copies of all payment confirmations — you'll need them for Dutch tax purposes.

Dutch Tax Considerations

Dutch residents who own property abroad must declare it to the Belastingdienst. The general principles:

Box 3 (Vermogensbelasting)

Your Spanish property is part of your Dutch Box 3 wealth declaration. The property's Spanish cadastral value (or market value, depending on circumstances) is included in your Box 3 assets. You may deduct any mortgage outstanding. The Spanish IBI you pay is not deductible against Dutch tax. The Netherlands-Spain double tax treaty determines which country has primary taxing rights over different income streams.

Rental income

If you rent the property (tourist rental or long-term), rental income is primarily taxed in Spain (at 19% for EU residents on net rental income). You must also declare this income in the Netherlands, but double-tax treaty provisions prevent double taxation — you'll generally receive a credit or exemption in the Netherlands for tax paid in Spain.

Capital gains on eventual sale

When you sell, capital gains are taxed in Spain (typically 19% for EU residents). Again, the double tax treaty applies. Your Dutch belastingadviseur should be consulted on how this interacts with your Dutch obligations.

Key Dutch tip: Spain has changed its non-resident tax rules significantly in recent years, and Dutch-Spanish cross-border taxation is more complex than many buyers expect. We see Dutch buyers regularly surprised by their ongoing Spanish tax obligations as non-residents. Engage a Dutch advisor with international property expertise before you buy, not after.

Mortgages for Dutch Buyers

Dutch buyers can obtain mortgages for Spanish property either through Dutch banks with Spanish operations or through Spanish banks directly. Both approaches work — the choice depends on rates, terms, and your circumstances.

Spanish mortgage

Spanish banks typically lend 60–70% of the appraised value to non-residents. Rates are currently variable or fixed in line with Euribor. Process typically takes 6–10 weeks. Sabadell and CaixaBank are commonly used by international buyers. You'll need to provide income documentation, tax returns (Dutch jaaropgave accepted), and bank statements.

Leveraging Dutch equity

Many Dutch buyers have significant equity in their Dutch homes. It's sometimes possible to extend a Dutch mortgage to release equity for a Spanish purchase. Discuss with your Dutch bank — not all will lend against foreign property, but some will.

Practical Considerations for Dutch Buyers

  • Flights: Transavia, Ryanair, and Vueling fly Amsterdam and Eindhoven to Girona (for Costa Brava) and Barcelona (for Maresme/Costa Dorada). Flight time approximately 2 hours. Many Costa Brava buyers find they're visiting more often than anticipated.
  • Community: There is a sizeable Dutch community along the Catalan Coast, particularly in the Maresme and Costa Brava. Dutch-speaking services (lawyers, gestors, estate agents) are available.
  • Language: Spanish and Catalan are the official languages. English is widely understood in the international buyer community. Dutch is less commonly spoken but not unusual in heavily Dutch areas. Learning some Spanish makes living here much more enjoyable.
  • DigiD and Dutch bureaucracy: You remain resident in the Netherlands during a holiday home purchase. You'll continue to use DigiD and file Dutch returns normally — simply adding your Spanish property and income to your declarations.

Why Dutch Buyers Choose the Catalan Coast

The Catalan Coast — particularly the Costa Brava — has been attracting Dutch buyers for decades. A few specific factors that resonate with Dutch buyers in our experience:

  • The landscape is rugged and wild rather than resort-manicured — more aesthetically aligned with Dutch sensibilities than the overdeveloped southern coasts
  • The cultural richness of Catalonia — food, architecture, music, local festivals — rewards curious buyers
  • The infrastructure is good without being overwhelming
  • Property values remain lower than comparable French or Italian coastal properties while being in a country with a robust legal system and EU membership
