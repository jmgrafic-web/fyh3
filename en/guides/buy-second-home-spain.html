Buying Guide

Buying a Second Home
in Spain

The complete guide — from choosing your region to signing the deed. What experienced buyers wish they'd known at the start.

Updated January 2025 18 min read

Spain is the most popular destination for European second-home buyers — and for good reason. The climate, the food, the cost of living relative to northern Europe, and the quality of life are genuinely exceptional. On the Catalan Coast in particular, you add world-class architecture, an extraordinary natural landscape, and a cultural richness that keeps owners coming back not just every summer but in spring, autumn, and increasingly, year-round.

But buying a second home in Spain is not a simple process — especially for international buyers. This guide covers the key decisions and the process, so you arrive at the transaction informed and protected.

The First Decision: Where?

Spain has many excellent second-home markets. The right region depends on your priorities — lifestyle, access from home, price, character of place, rental potential. Here's how the Catalan Coast compares to the alternatives buyers typically consider:

Costa Brava vs Costa del Sol

The Costa del Sol offers more infrastructure, more English speakers, and established resort infrastructure. The Costa Brava offers better natural scenery, more authentic Catalan culture, stricter planning (which protects values), and a more discerning buyer community. Prices are comparable; character is very different.

Costa Brava vs South of France

The French Riviera has comparable beauty and often higher prices. Taxes are higher in France. The legal process is different. Many buyers who've considered both find the Catalan coast offers better value and a slightly warmer reception to foreign buyers.

Costa Brava vs Mallorca / Ibiza

The islands offer different infrastructure and are more accessible by flight from more European cities. But the mainland coast offers better connectivity by road, more diverse landscapes, and typically lower prices per square metre for comparable quality.

Maresme vs Costa Brava

The Maresme is 30–40 minutes from Barcelona by train — genuinely urban-adjacent. Costa Brava is more remote and more natural. If year-round use and Barcelona access matter, Maresme is compelling. If escape and nature are the priority, Costa Brava wins.

Setting Your Budget Correctly

The most common mistake: budgeting for the property price only. In Catalonia, purchase costs add approximately 12–15% to the headline price. On a €600,000 property, that's €72,000–€90,000 in taxes and fees. This is not negotiable.

Your total budget must include:

  • Purchase price
  • ITP (Transfer Tax) — 10% in Catalonia
  • Notary and Land Registry fees — approximately 0.3–0.5%
  • Lawyer fees — typically 0.5–1%
  • Architectural assessment — €1,800–€3,500
  • Renovation reserve — if buying a property requiring work, budget conservatively
  • Furniture and fitting out — often underestimated

The Questions to Ask Before You Buy

How often will you realistically use it?

Most buyers overestimate usage in the first years of ownership and underestimate it later. Be honest about this: a property you use 4 weeks per year has very different requirements than one you use 16 weeks. This affects the type and location of property that makes most sense.

Will you rent it when you're not there?

Tourist rental licences (llicència turística) are required in Catalonia and are now strictly limited in supply. In some areas, moratoriums on new licences mean that if the property you're buying doesn't already have a licence, you may not be able to obtain one. This dramatically affects rental income potential — verify the licence status before making any offer.

What's your renovation tolerance?

Properties needing work offer character and often better locations than newer builds. But renovation in Spain is slower, more expensive, and more complicated than northern European buyers typically expect. If you're buying to renovate, work with an architect from the start — not as an afterthought.

What happens in 10–15 years?

Think about resale. Properties in attractive locations with good access, existing tourist licences, and architectural quality hold and grow in value. Properties in secondary locations, with access limitations or planning complications, are much harder to sell.

The Legal Process: What to Expect

The Spanish purchase process is different from most northern European systems. Here's the sequence:

  1. Find the property — through an agent, off-market, or via our search service
  2. Due diligence — legal checks by your lawyer, architectural assessment by us
  3. Contrato de arras — private contract with 10% deposit, subject to conditions
  4. Mortgage approval (if applicable) — typically 6–10 weeks
  5. Escritura pública — signing before a notary, full payment, keys exchanged
  6. Post-completion — ITP paid, property registered in Land Registry

The typical timeline from offer to completion is 6–12 weeks for a cash purchase, 10–16 weeks if financing is involved.

Owning and Managing Your Second Home

Once you've bought, the ongoing management needs to be considered:

Property management

Most second-home owners use a local property manager who handles key exchanges, cleaning, maintenance, and emergency response. A good property manager is one of the most valuable relationships you'll build. Ask for references and check how many properties they manage — both too few and too many can be problems.

Maintenance budget

Budget 1–2% of property value per year for maintenance. Pools, gardens, appliances, and the general wear of a property used intensively in summer and left empty in winter require consistent attention. Deferred maintenance is the primary way properties deteriorate in value.

Tax filings

As a non-resident property owner in Spain, you are obligated to file an annual Spanish tax return (Modelo 210) even if you have no rental income. This is a simple filing — a Spanish gestor handles it for €100–€200/year — but it's mandatory. Non-compliance creates administrative problems when you eventually sell.

The second home as investment: Many buyers ask about return on investment. Well-chosen properties on the Catalan Coast have shown strong capital appreciation over the past decade — Costa Brava in particular. Rental yields (net, after management and costs) typically run 3–5% on well-managed properties. The combination of yield and appreciation has delivered strong total returns for well-informed buyers. However, property is illiquid and individual results vary significantly by location, condition, and licence status.

Common Mistakes — and How to Avoid Them

  • Falling in love with a view and ignoring the structure. Commission the architectural assessment. Every time.
  • Buying without a tourist rental licence when rental income matters. Check the licence status before any offer. If no licence exists, get a written assessment of whether one can be obtained.
  • Using the seller's lawyer. Your lawyer represents you only. Never use the same lawyer as the seller.
  • Not budgeting for renovation realistically. Spain's renovation costs are well-documented. Read our renovation cost guide before you budget.
  • Ignoring ongoing tax obligations. Spanish non-resident tax, wealth tax (for larger portfolios), and home country obligations all need to be understood before purchase, not after.
  • Choosing location for price alone. The best-value properties on secondary streets, facing north, without sea views or easy beach access, are cheap for reasons that don't go away.
